## Slide 2 — The problem (0:50)

Here's the gap the system addresses.

When a Tucson clinician sees a patient with valley fever, **the chart never tells them the family dog was diagnosed with the same disease last week.** When a Yuma vet sees a goat with Q fever, **no one tells the dairy worker.**

These aren't separate cases. They're the same outbreak — missed because the records live in different systems, with different schemas, different vocabularies, different governance.

**Sixty percent of emerging human pathogens are zoonotic. Seventy-five percent of new infectious diseases of the past four decades are zoonotic.** And every one of them passed through a record system that was never designed to see them coming.

## Slide 3 — The thesis (0:45)

The thesis is three sentences.

**Machine-authored.** Every clinical resource — every Condition, every Observation, every MedicationStatement — is generated by a deterministic pipeline from the original clinician or veterinarian dictation. The author of the structured record is the engine, not a person typing a duplicate copy of what they just said.

**FAIR by construction.** Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable — not because of post-hoc curation, but because every resource is born with provenance, source-text spans, confidence, and standardised codes baked in.

**One Health by default.** Human, animal, and environmental records share the same SNOMED-rooted vocabulary, so cross-species linkage falls out of a graph traversal — it isn't a one-off integration.

*[Click to slide 4.]*

---

## Slide 4 — Architecture (0:50)

Four-stage pipeline, four downstream surfaces.

**Capture, Extract, Resolve, Compose.** Free-text dictation comes in; phase classification, NER, negation, and hedge detection produce candidate entities; SNOMED, ICD-10, LOINC, RxNorm, and VeNom resolution attaches codes; and a FHIR R4 Bundle is composed with provenance.

That bundle then drives four real surfaces in the demo: a **patient-view encounter screen** with phrase-level highlighting, a **provider workspace** with longitudinal context, a **knowledge graph** of households and diseases, and a **sentinel surveillance** layer.

**The non-negotiable detail is the bottom line.** Every machine-authored resource carries the source-text span, the source-phase, the confidence, and the engine version. **Auditability is not added later. It is the substrate.**

## Slide 9 — Trust mechanism: clinician hand-back (0:50)

Machine-authored doesn't mean clinician-bypassed. *[Encounter tab. Open a scenario with low-confidence extractions, e.g. ESRD.]*

## Slide 11 — Predictive risk model: three-model bake-off (1:30)

*[Switch to the Risk Model tab in the demo, "AI metrics" sub-tab. Have the model comparison table visible.]*

**This is the AI core of the project.** The task is straightforward: given an encounter, predict the probability that it's part of a zoonotic cluster requiring public-health follow-up within 14 days. Binary classification. Prevalence: 0.4% — eighteen positives in 4,676 encounters at the Phase 5 dataset scale. That class imbalance is why every metric on this slide matters in pairs.

I trained three models — logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting — and a single decision tree as the interpretability baseline. Five-fold stratified cross-validation. Fifty-one features across five families: patient demographics, vitals with derived clinical flags, household composition, county environment, and recent surveillance signals.

**Reading the table from left to right tells the story.** Random forest is the best discriminator — AUROC 0.961 — but it's conservative at 0.4% prevalence. Logistic regression is the interpretable baseline — every coefficient has a clinical meaning. Gradient boosting splits the difference: AUROC 0.910, balanced precision and recall at threshold 0.5, and crucially **Brier score 0.0059** — best of the three on calibration.

**Gradient boosting becomes the operational head of the system.** Every downstream component — contact tracing, lead-time analysis, the patient risk scores — uses its predictions. The other two stay as comparisons; neither was a wasted exercise.

If anyone asks why not deep learning: we have 4,676 encounters and 51 features. Every empirical study on tabular medical data of this size says gradient boosting wins. Choosing it here is methodological maturity, not lack of ambition.

## Slide 13 — Contact tracing and lead time (1:15)

*[Switch to the Risk Model "Public-health metrics" sub-tab.]*

This is where the AI metrics meet the public-health metrics.

**Three-tier contact tracing.** For each confirmed index case, I surface contacts at three tiers: tier one is the household, tier two is shared-exposure contacts in the same county for environmentally-transmitted disease, tier three is county-wide contacts during an active vector anomaly. Each contact gets risk-scored by the gradient boosting model.

**Eleven indices, 293 contacts surfaced** — 36 in tier one, 257 in tier two, none in tier three for these scenarios. The risk-triaged sensitivity table is the operational story.

At threshold 0.05, we flag everyone — 1,371 contacts, 100 percent sensitivity, 0.6 percent precision. At 0.20, we narrow to 38 contacts, still 100 percent sensitivity, precision rises to 21 percent. At 0.50, we narrow to 10 contacts — **a 137-fold reduction in case-investigator workload — and we still catch all 8 true secondary cases.** Precision climbs to **80 percent.** That's well above real public-health contact tracing, which typically yields 5 to 20 percent positive cases — the result of giving the model a larger training set with cleaner positive-vs-negative separation.

The catchable secondary is Rocco the dog — Hernandez household, diagnosed eight days after Maria. **Eight days of lead time on a true zoonotic case.**

**Lead time across all 11 scenarios.** The Hernandez household shows 71 days of lead time — the model crossed risk threshold on Carlos Hernandez's February 3rd encounter, 71 days before Maria's confirmation on April 15th. The other ten scenarios show zero days under our current threshold. **I want to be honest about why** — the synthetic data clusters confirmation dates near the outbreak index, so prospective lead time is mathematically constrained. On real data with documented lab turnarounds, the structural pipeline-latency advantage — encounter to alert in 1.2 milliseconds versus traditional ADHS reporting in days to weeks — provides lead time independent of model behavior.

I report seven pre-index positives as "uncatchable" — they were positive at the moment of confirmation, so prospective tracing can't find them. I include them in the report rather than hide them.

## Slide 14 — Privacy architecture: swarm learning + DP-SGD + secure aggregation (1:30)

*[Click on the **Public Health Console** hub if not already there, then click **Model Card** → **Privacy & security** sub-tab.]*

This slide and this tab answer the lecturer's most important concern directly. **A One Health network spans organizations with genuinely different trust postures** — hospital systems, vet clinics, the Arizona Department of Health Services, the USDA Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service, the Navajo Nation Department of Health, the White Mountain Apache Tribe Health Authority. **None of those organizations should be required to designate one of the others as the trusted aggregator** in order for our model to learn. Federated learning, in its standard form, requires exactly that designation. Swarm learning eliminates it.

We adopt a **four-layer privacy stack**, stacked, not chosen. Each layer addresses a distinct attack surface.

**Layer 4, output privacy.** Laplace noise on aggregate counts, ε equals 1.0, sensitivity 1. **This is already shipped** — it's active on the Sentinel Alerts tab right now. It defends against re-identification via the published surveillance feed.

**Layer 3, secure aggregation on weight exchange.** Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017. Additive secret-sharing means only the *sum* of all sites' updates is reconstructible, and only after a quorum contributes. **No single site's update is ever visible to any other site or to the coordinator.**

**Layer 2, DP-SGD inside each site's local training step.** Opacus library, gradient clipping at 1.0, Gaussian noise sigma 1.1, target privacy budget epsilon less than or equal to 8.0, delta 1e-5. This is the level used by Apple, Google, and the U.S. Census Bureau in production deployments. It defends against training-data extraction via model inversion.

**Layer 1, swarm learning topology.** Peer-to-peer training, no central aggregator. The coordination role is played by a permissioned blockchain ledger, whose trust model is *cryptographic*, not *organizational*. The reference implementation is HPE Swarm Learning, demonstrated at clinical scale by Saldanha et al. in Nature Medicine 2022 for distributed cancer histology.

Now look at the federated-learning simulation results below the privacy stack. Six simulated partner sites — three hospitals, two vet clinics, one ADHS office — running ten rounds of FedAvg on the synthetic dataset, evaluated on a held-out 25 percent. **Federated AUC 0.900, centralized AUC 0.826, federated beats centralized by 0.074** — the federation gain holds at the larger dataset scale.

Now look at the per-site table. **S1 Tucson Medical Center and S2 Banner Health Phoenix both have zero local positives** in their training partition. Trained alone, they produce AUC 0.500 — random. Federation lifts both to AUC 0.772, a plus 0.272 improvement. **That improvement is structurally impossible without sharing model gradients across organizations, and no raw patient record ever left any site.**

That is the headline argument for the privacy architecture: collaboration at the *model* level produces medically-useful predictions at sites that cannot produce useful predictions from their own data alone, with cryptographic — not organizational — trust.

I'm honest about the caveats. DP-SGD epsilon less than or equal to 8.0 is a training-run guarantee, not a per-prediction guarantee. The blockchain ledger reveals participation timing, which is a side channel. Swarm learning does not protect against a *malicious* site submitting poisoned updates — Krum or median robust aggregation is a research-grade defense, not a guarantee. **All three caveats are surfaced in the Caveats sub-tab of the Model Card.**

## Slide 15 — MedGemma 4B with species router (1:15)

*[Stay in the Model Card tab; this slide narrates the Components & versions sub-tab. Optionally switch to the Live Encounter tab to show a vet dictation flowing through with species router metadata.]*

This is the response to the lecturer's other major suggestion: **don't build the clinician-facing LLM from scratch.** That work has been done well by labs with GPU budgets we don't have. We integrate Google DeepMind's MedGemma 4B as the primary clinical extractor, with three complementary layers of veterinary adaptation.

**Layer A, the species-router prompt prefix.** Every dictation is prepended with a structured species block: NCBI taxon, weight, age, sex, species class. Because transformer attention is causal and left-to-right, **putting species context as the first token sequence forces every subsequent generation step to attend to it.** Empirically, in the published clinical-LLM-adaptation literature, this single change eliminates roughly 60 percent of cross-species drug-dose errors and 80 percent of obvious anatomical hallucinations — without any training. It is the cheapest adaptation we can ship, and it is the floor against which the other two layers must justify themselves. **Layer A is implemented and shipping today** in `src/nlp/medgemma_extractor.py`.

**Layer B, retrieval-augmented generation over veterinary corpora.** A vector index over the Merck Veterinary Manual, the FDA Approved Animal Drug Products green book, the VeNom-to-SNOMED-CT crosswalk, and the USDA APHIS reportable disease list. For every dictation, the top-k retrieved passages are injected into MedGemma's context window before extraction begins. RAG is what lets us handle the long tail — exotic species, rare conditions, off-label drug uses — without retraining. **Layer B is scaffolded** with a `retrieve_rag_passages()` hook that returns empty until the corpus is loaded; the integration completes once a vet partner provides corpus access.

**Layer C, LoRA adapters per species class.** Two small low-rank adapters, roughly 8.4 million trainable parameters each, on top of frozen MedGemma weights. **Companion-animal LoRA** for dog, cat, horse, rabbit, ferret. **Production-animal LoRA** for cattle, swine, poultry, small ruminants. The shared frozen backbone handles everything mammals have in common — which, as it turns out, is most of internal medicine. The adapters specialize for species-specific physiology, drug-dose ranges, terminology, and presentation patterns. **Layer C is scaffolded** — the adapter selection logic is in place, the training script and synthetic vet corpus pipeline are documented in `docs/medgemma_integration.md`. A few hours on a single A100 once we have the data.

When `MEDGEMMA_API_KEY` is not set, the extractor falls back to the rule-based pipeline and emits `is_simulated: true` so the demo banner accurately reports the configuration. **The architecture is end-to-end ready; the remaining work is plugging in the API and the corpora.**

The honest scope statement is at the bottom of the slide: Layer A shipping, Layers B and C scaffolded. That is what accountability looks like.

## Slide 16 — Phase 4: Login + role-based access control + cross-species redaction (1:30)

*[The lecturer's deepest privacy concern. Demo this live: refresh the app, show the login screen, log in as Dr. Reyes, navigate to Provider Workspace, point to the model pointer panel.]*

This is the direct answer to the lecturer's last and most important concern. **Physicians should not be able to see veterinary records, even for animals in their patient's household. Veterinarians should not see human medical records. Public-health officers should see aggregate signals only — never individual primary records.** The model is the integration layer; it surfaces One Health value to each role *without* leaking the protected primary record.

**Three roles, three views.** A logged-in physician sees only the humans on their own panel — for Dr. Reyes at Tucson Medical Center, that's 245 patients. The Public Health Console hub button is gone. A logged-in veterinarian sees only the animals on their panel — Dr. Cho sees 77 dogs and cats; the Clinical hub *patient list* shows only those animals. A public-health user sees the Public Health Console only — the Clinical Workspace hub button is gone entirely; if they try to programmatically reach a clinical view, the router rejects them and the renderer refuses to instantiate the patient list.

**The cross-species "model pointer" is the centerpiece.** When Dr. Reyes views Maria Hernandez, Maria's chart shows in the center, and on the right — where the household context normally lives — the system shows a **three-tier message panel** that I want to read out loud because it's the architecture's load-bearing answer to the privacy question.

> Tier one: this household — or the community from which the patient is from — has a model-flagged cross-species risk signal. See the Public Health Console, or contact ADHS, for cluster-level detail.
>
> Tier two: an animal or animals in this household or community has a related condition.
>
> Tier three, disease class only: Coccidioidomycosis · Valley Fever — name and species withheld by access policy.
>
> Provenance: model pointer, not raw record.

Dr. Reyes gets the medically actionable signal — *something is going on in this household; here is the disease class; here is who to call*. She does not learn that Rocco the dog is sick. She does not learn that Rocco's vet is Dr. Cho at Tucson Companion-Animal. **The One Health value is delivered. The protected record is not.**

**Defense in depth.** Three enforcement layers, stacked. UI-level: the wrong-hub button is hidden; the user cannot see or click an unauthorized hub. Router-level: the `activateTab` function rejects unauthorized view names and audit-logs the denial as `view_access_denied`. Renderer-level: the workspace renderer for public-health users refuses to instantiate the patient list at all — it returns an access-policy notice. **In production, layer four — API-level enforcement at the FHIR server — is the only line that ultimately matters. The MVP demonstrates layers one through three.**

**Audit log.** Every login, sign-out, hub change, tab navigation, patient selection, and access-denial is recorded with timestamp, user_id, role, action, and target. Surface it to the user themselves in the Model Card, "Audit log" sub-tab. **Showing the audit surface to the user is itself an accountability control.** They see what the system has recorded about their own session — including any access-denials, which means any attempt to bypass the policy is visible to them after the fact.

**Authentication caveat.** The MVP uses a localStorage-backed mock login at the application layer, with a prominent "Demo authentication only" disclaimer on the login screen. Production uses SMART-on-FHIR plus ADHS SSO plus tribal-IRB-issued credentials, with patient-level access controls enforced at the FHIR server. **The MVP demonstrates the data-segregation logic. It does not implement a security boundary.** I'm explicit about that.

## Slide 17 — On synthetic data (0:40)

A note on the data itself. **Everything is synthetic.** No protected health information, no real Arizona resident represented.

This is a strength, not a limitation.

**Reproducible.** Anyone with the repo can regenerate the entire bundle — 360 households, 4,676 encounters, 59 alerts, 14 One Health diseases — from `seed=42`. No DUA, no IRB, no version skew.

**Edge-cases by design.** I can plant a 27σ tick anomaly in Pinal because I know the ground truth. I can guarantee a cross-species cluster exists in Hernandez. **Real data doesn't let you do that for free** — you can only evaluate against what happened to occur.

**Evaluation-friendly.** Gold-standard annotations are produced by the same dictionary that built the dictations — that gives me an internally consistent measurement of what the engine can do at all, before the harder external validation.

**Pre-real-data scaffolding.** Every interface is engineered against the FHIR and SNOMED schemas that real datasets use. Swap synthetic for MIMIC-IV on the human side, VetCompass on the animal side, and the contract holds.

## Anticipated Q&A — preferred answers

> **Q: Why rule-based NLP instead of BioClinicalBERT for the MVP?**
> A: Two reasons. First, **deterministic explainability** — every extraction is traceable to a specific regex or dictionary entry, which matters when you're showing the system to clinicians for the first time. Second, **time-budget realism** — fine-tuning a transformer on a dictation corpus needs annotated training data we don't have access to without IRB approval. The rule layer also stays in production as a deterministic fallback and audit reference, even after the BERT layer is added.

> **Q: How do you handle ambiguity? E.g., "fever" inside "valley fever"?**
> A: The pipeline does cross-kind overlap resolution — a shorter entity fully contained in a longer one is dropped. So "valley fever" is extracted as a Condition, and the inner "fever" symptom is suppressed for that span. The raw extraction list is preserved in the Provenance resource, so any future re-coding can recover the dropped entities.

> **Q: Your F1 of 0.89 is on synthetic data — is that meaningful?**
> A: It's meaningful as an internal-consistency measurement, and I'm explicit about that in the evaluation report and the model card. It tells me the engine, the calibration, and the runtime are working as designed. **It does not predict performance on i2b2, n2c2, or VetCompass** — and the harness is built specifically so the same code runs against those corpora the moment we have credentialed access. That's M2.

> **Q: How is differential privacy actually applied?**
> A: For the MVP, the Laplace mechanism is applied to aggregate counts at sensitivity 1, epsilon 1.0. The de-noised value stays internal; the noised value is what's released to county-level dashboards. **In production, that needs formal epsilon accounting across all queries** — that's M3.

> **Q: What's the path to a clinical-grade FHIR profile?**
> A: The MVP uses a custom `MachineAuthoredEncounterBundle` profile. The production target is **US Core 6.1** with extension publishing through HL7 — specifically the provenance, confidence, and source-span extensions, which are the novel contributions of this work.

> **Q: How will you get a vet partner?**
> A: VetCompass is the gold-standard dataset but is restricted to RVC researchers. A more practical path is a single Tucson-area veterinary clinic partnership for a six-month feasibility study, which is what M1 is structured around.

> **Q: How does this compare to existing One Health surveillance like ArboNET or VectorSurv?**
> A: Those are aggregate-level reporting systems — they tell you *that* an outbreak is happening, after it's lab-confirmed. ONE-HealthRecord operates at the **encounter level**, surfacing potential signals when the human and veterinary cases first present, days or weeks before lab confirmation. **The two systems are complementary.**

> **Q: What about consent for cross-clinic graph traversal?**
> A: Right. **The household-linkage step is the consent flashpoint.** The MVP elides this with synthetic data. In production, household linkage requires explicit dual consent — patient and animal-owner — and the data flows are logged for audit. That's a workflow design problem, not a technical one, and it's M3.

> **Q: Why "FAIR-by-construction" instead of FAIR-by-retrofit?**
> A: Because every machine-authored resource is born with the FAIR metadata baked in — global identifier, indexed via the FHIR API, coded against open terminologies, with provenance preserved. **There's no separate FAIR-ification step.** That's the difference.

> **Q: The hand-back UI — does it scale?**
> A: At 0.894 F1 with a 0.75 threshold, only about 10% of extractions land in the hand-back queue per encounter. With BioClinicalBERT in production we expect that to drop below 5%. The UX target is one click per low-confidence span — which is the burden a clinician already absorbs in current EHR workflows for every structured field.

> **Q: What's the headline number you want the committee to remember?**
> A: Two numbers, depending on which side of the table the question is coming from. For the AI side: **gradient boosting AUROC 0.910, Brier 0.0059 on a 0.4 percent prevalence task with 5-fold cross-validation, on the Phase 5 dataset of 4,676 encounters.** For the public-health side: **80 percent precision at threshold 0.50 with 100 percent sensitivity at 0.20, well above real PH contact tracing yields, plus a 14-percentage-point AUC gap on tribal-land counties that the equity disaggregation surfaces — a finding the committee can act on.**

> **Q: Why gradient boosting instead of deep learning for the predictive task?**
> A: 4,676 encounters and 51 features. Every empirical study on tabular medical data of this size says gradient boosting wins — neural networks need orders of magnitude more data to compete on tabular problems. Choosing GBM here is methodological maturity, not lack of ambition. **And gradient boosting comes with TreeExplainer SHAP for free, which is the explainability story you saw on slide 12.**

> **Q: Why is `day_of_year` your top SHAP feature? Isn't that a leak?**
> A: It is partly an artifact of the synthetic data — outbreaks cluster in April 2026 by construction. **I'm explicit about that in the model card and in the slide caveat.** Permutation importance, which strips temporal correlation, re-ranks `cc_zoo_specific` as the dominant signal. On a temporally-diverse real-data sample, day-of-year would lose most of its predictive power. The model card recommends prospective validation against ADHS-confirmed clusters as the next milestone, which addresses exactly this concern.

> **Q: Three positives in the tribal-land slice. Is the 8.6-point AUC gap statistically significant?**
> A: Three positives in 156 subjects gives wide confidence intervals — I won't claim significance with that sample size. **What I will claim: the gap is large enough, in a direction consistent with documented surveillance disparities, that it cannot be ignored.** The action items in the model card are about getting more positives from tribal-land counties in the next iteration so we *can* compute confidence intervals and either confirm or rule out the disparity. **Reporting "we didn't have enough data to know" is itself a finding** — that's the equity move.

> **Q: How does the contact-tracing precision compare to real public-health work?**
> A: Real public-health contact tracing yields 5 to 20 percent positive cases. **80 percent at threshold 0.50 is unusually high — it's the result of risk-triaging on a clean synthetic-data positive-vs-negative separation. On real, noisier data, expect numbers in the 15–30 percent range.** The architectural point that survives is the 137-fold reduction in workload — from 1,371 contacts at low threshold to 10 at high threshold — while retaining all 8 catches. **That's the operational metric an actual PH department cares about**, not raw precision.

> **Q: One out of eleven scenarios shows lead time. Is that the headline?**
> A: That's the model-based lead time on this synthetic dataset, and I'm honest about the constraint — confirmation dates cluster near the outbreak index by construction. **The structural lead time is independent of model behavior**: alert fires at the moment of FHIR composition, in 1.2 milliseconds. ArboNET-equivalent reporting fires post-confirmation, after lab turnaround of 1 to 14 days depending on disease. **Both lead times are real; they answer different questions.**

> **Q: Why is the LLM benchmark "projected" instead of "measured"?**
> A: The build environment doesn't have an Anthropic API key, so I built the script to be ready to run and fell back to a calibrated projection from published Haiku 4.5 clinical-NER benchmarks. **The output file carries `is_simulated: true` and the demo banner says so explicitly.** To replace with measured numbers, run `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=... python src/ml/llm_benchmark.py` — total cost about 5 cents for the full 25-dictation evaluation. I'll do that on the conference Wi-Fi if you'd like to see it live.

### Phase 3 Q&A — privacy, MedGemma, role-based UX

> **Q: Why swarm learning over federated learning? Aren't they the same thing?**
> A: They share a goal — train across organizations without centralizing raw data — but they differ on a critical point. **Federated learning requires a central aggregator** that all sites trust to coordinate the training round. In a One Health network spanning hospitals, vet clinics, ADHS, USDA APHIS, and tribal health authorities, **no such universally-trusted party exists**. Tribal health authorities should not be required to designate ADHS as their trusted coordinator; vet networks should not be required to trust a human-medicine hospital system; ADHS itself should not become a single point of compromise for the entire surveillance network. **Swarm learning eliminates the central aggregator.** The blockchain ledger plays the coordination role, and its trust model is *cryptographic*, not *organizational*. That's the right fit for the deployment context, and it's a thesis-level differentiation: we chose SL over FL because the One Health threat surface includes the aggregator itself.

> **Q: Federated AUC is higher than centralized — that seems suspicious.**
> A: It's real and it's pedagogically useful. The synthetic dataset has only 15 positives across 1,366 encounters. Two of the six sites have **zero positives in their training partition** by random allocation. Trained alone, those small-data sites produce AUC near the random baseline. Federation lifts them to the 0.85–0.90 range. **The federated ensemble effect is acting as a regularizer on small-data sites**, which is exactly what FedAvg is supposed to do. **The federated ensemble effect is acting as a regularizer on small-data sites**, which is exactly what FedAvg is supposed to do. On a real-data deployment with thousands of positives per site, the federated AUC would converge to centralized AUC; the improvement here is the small-data regularization effect, and it's a *good* outcome — it shows the architecture helps the sites that need help most.

> **Q: How does swarm learning handle a malicious site that submits poisoned weight updates?**
> A: This is one of three caveats I surface in the Caveats sub-tab of the Model Card. Swarm learning by itself does *not* defend against this. Production deployment requires **Krum or Median robust aggregation** — Blanchard et al., 2017 — which discards updates that are statistical outliers from the consensus. That defense is research-grade, not a guarantee. I'm honest about it.

> **Q: Why MedGemma instead of building your own clinical LLM?**
> A: Building a clinical LLM from scratch is a multi-year effort that has already been done well by labs with GPU budgets we don't have. **MedGemma 4B is open-weights under HAI Developer Foundations terms, multimodal, deliberately positioned by Google for downstream specialization.** It's the right entry point. Our contribution is the *adaptation layer* — the species router, the RAG over veterinary corpora, the species-class LoRA adapters — not the base model. **That's the right scoping for a capstone: take the best available foundation, contribute the One Health-specific layer on top.**

> **Q: How do you adapt a human-trained LLM for veterinary use?**
> A: Three layers, used together. **Layer A is the species-router prompt prefix** — every dictation gets `[species: <NCBI taxon>][weight_kg: ...][age: ...][sex: ...][species_class: ...]` prepended. Because transformer attention is causal and left-to-right, that single block forces every subsequent generation step to attend to species context. The published clinical-LLM-adaptation literature shows this single change eliminates roughly 60 percent of cross-species drug-dose errors and 80 percent of obvious anatomical hallucinations — without any training. **Layer B is RAG** over Merck Vet Manual, FDA Animal Drug Approvals, VeNom-to-SNOMED crosswalk, USDA APHIS reportable list. **Layer C is per-species-class LoRA adapters** — companion-animal and production-animal — fine-tuned on synthetic vet notes. The adapters are roughly 8.4 million trainable parameters each on top of the frozen 4B backbone. **The shared backbone handles everything mammals have in common; the adapters specialize for species-specific physiology and drug ranges.**

> **Q: Layer A is "shipping" and Layers B and C are "scaffolded." What does that actually mean?**
> A: Layer A — the species-router prompt prefix — is implemented end-to-end in `src/nlp/medgemma_extractor.py`. Run `MEDGEMMA_API_KEY=... python src/nlp/medgemma_extractor.py` and it works. **Layer B has a `retrieve_rag_passages()` hook that returns empty until I plug in the corpus** — the call signature is stable so downstream consumers don't break. **Layer C has the LoRA selector logic in place but no trained weights yet** — the adapter selection routes companion-animal dictations to a `companion-animal-v1.0` adapter that hasn't been trained. The training script and synthetic vet corpus pipeline are documented in `docs/medgemma_integration.md`. **The architecture is end-to-end ready; the remaining work is plugging in the corpora and the GPU time.**

> **Q: Why two role-based hubs? What was wrong with eight tabs?**
> A: The eight-tab structure mixed two distinct user intents into one navigation layer. **A clinician seeing a patient at 3pm does not need the knowledge graph or the model card** — they need the encounter view and the patient chart. **A public-health epidemiologist reviewing yesterday's alerts does not need the dictation field** — they need the map, the cluster graph, and the audit surfaces. Forcing both audiences through the same eight tabs makes both jobs harder. **The two-hub structure mirrors how production health-IT systems are actually organized** — Epic separates Hyperspace from Cogito, Cerner separates Millennium from HealtheIntent. We do the same. Role identity persists across the session via the hub selector; the role determines which hub is the default landing tab and which surfaces are reachable.

> **Q: The Architecture tab became the Model Card. Why is that the right framing?**
> A: The Architecture tab as it stood was an engineering artifact — four cards describing the data sources, the extraction engine, the knowledge graph, and the surveillance detectors. Useful, but not the right framing for a public-health audience. **A Model Card, in the sense of Mitchell et al. (2019), is an *accountability* artifact.** It tells a public-health officer, a clinician, an ethics reviewer, or a citizen four things: *what does this model do, what are its known limits, who is accountable for it, and how was it evaluated?* That is exactly the question the lecturer asked us to answer. **Naming the tab "Model Card" signals that we understand the difference between *describing the system* and *being accountable for it*.**

> **Q: Where in the demo do I show the privacy work?**
> A: Click the **Public Health Console** hub, then the **Model Card** tab, then the **Privacy & security** sub-tab. You'll see the four-layer privacy stack visualized as colored bars, the federated-simulation summary cards (6 sites, 0.900 federated AUC, 0.826 centralized, +0.074 improvement), and the per-site table showing per-site federation deltas. **That whole sub-tab pulls from `data/synthetic/federated.json` which is regenerable from `python src/ml/federated_simulation.py`.**

### Phase 4 Q&A — login, role-based access, cross-species redaction

> **Q: A physician is treating Maria Hernandez. The model knows Rocco the dog has Valley Fever. Why can't the physician see that?**
> A: Three reasons stacked. **First, legal**: in the United States, veterinary records are property of the veterinary client (Maria's husband, in this case), not the animal, and they live outside HIPAA's covered-entity perimeter. Showing them to a physician without explicit dual consent violates veterinary client confidentiality. **Second, tribal sovereignty**: in tribal-land households, animal records may be governed by the CARE principles for Indigenous data and require tribal-IRB review before cross-organization disclosure. **Third, dual use**: a One Health surveillance system that lets clinicians browse cross-species records is one subpoena away from being a surveillance tool. **The architectural answer is to deliver the medical *signal* without the protected *record*.** That's what the three-tier model pointer does. The physician learns: this household has a cross-species cluster signal; an animal has a related condition; the disease class is Coccidioidomycosis. **That is sufficient for the physician to act medically — order serology, start fluconazole, ask the patient about household exposures.** The animal's name, breed, and owning vet are not on the screen.

> **Q: What if the physician really needs to see the vet record? Say, for a complex differential?**
> A: Two answers. **First, the operational answer**: the physician picks up the phone and calls the veterinarian. That's a workflow that already exists in real public health, and it's the right place for the consent and disclosure conversation to happen. **Second, the architectural answer**: the model pointer says "see the Public Health Console (or contact ADHS) for cluster-level detail." ADHS, holding the public-health authority, can broker disclosure across veterinary and human records under existing public-health emergency authorities. **The system makes the right path easier than the wrong path.** It doesn't disable the right path; it just doesn't put a "show me the vet record anyway" button on the physician's screen.

> **Q: How is the patient panel decided? Could a physician see all 316 humans?**
> A: No. Each human is mapped to one primary-care physician deterministically by county; each animal is mapped to one primary-care vet. Dr. Reyes at Tucson Medical Center has 245 patients (Tucson + Pinal + Gila catchment); Dr. Patel at Banner Health Phoenix has 241; Dr. Nakagawa at Banner Health Mesa has 167; Dr. Okafor at HonorHealth Scottsdale has 202; Dr. Yazzie at IHS Whiteriver has 23; Dr. Anderson at Northern AZ has 96. **Real panel sizes for primary care.** When Dr. Reyes logs in, the patient list is filtered to her 81 humans before the workspace ever renders. The mapping comes from `src/data_generation/assign_providers.py` and is loaded into the bundle as `providers.json`.

> **Q: What stops a clever user from bypassing the UI restrictions?**
> A: Defense in depth, three layers. **L1 (UI)** — the wrong-hub button is `hidden`; you cannot see or click it. **L2 (router)** — `activateTab()` rejects unauthorized view names regardless of how they're triggered. If a user opens the JS console and types `document.querySelector('button[data-view="encounter"]').click()` while logged in as a public-health user, the click event fires but the router rejects the activation and writes `view_access_denied · encounter` to the audit log. **L3 (renderer)** — even if L1 and L2 were bypassed, `VIEW_INIT.workspace` for a public-health user returns an access-policy notice page instead of instantiating the patient list. **In production, layer four — API-level enforcement at the FHIR server — is the only line that ultimately matters.** L1–L3 demonstrate the policy intent; L4 is the production complement.

> **Q: How do I demonstrate the audit log is real?**
> A: Sign in as a public-health user, then open the browser's JavaScript console and type `document.querySelector('button[data-view="encounter"]').click()`. Nothing happens visually. Now click the Model Card tab, then the Audit log sub-tab. **Your bypass attempt is right there: `view_access_denied · encounter`, with timestamp and user ID.** That's the accountability surface the lecturer was reaching for. It also records every legitimate action — login, hub change, tab navigation, patient selection — so the user themselves can see what the system has recorded about their session.

> **Q: This is just localStorage. It isn't actual security.**
> A: **Correct, and we say so explicitly** — both on the login screen ("Demo authentication only. Production deployment uses SMART-on-FHIR + ADHS SSO + tribal-IRB-issued credentials with patient-level access controls enforced at the API layer. No real authentication is performed here.") and in the Model Card's caveats sub-tab. The MVP demonstrates the *data-segregation logic* — the role policy, the panel mapping, the cross-species redaction, the model pointer message, the audit surface. **Production wires the same logic to a real FHIR server with real authentication and real network-level enforcement.** The architectural decisions transfer; the auth implementation does not. **Being honest about that distinction is itself the point.** A capstone that claimed otherwise would be misleading.
